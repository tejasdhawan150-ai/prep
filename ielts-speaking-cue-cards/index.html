IELTS Speaking Cue Cards with Sample Answers

In IELTS Speaking Part 2 you get a cue card, one minute to plan and up to two minutes to speak. Below are common cue card topics with original band 8 model answers, plus a simple method to structure any card.

Last updated: 7 October 2026 · Written by the PrepEve team

How to answer a Part 2 cue card

  • Use the full minute of planning. Write 4–5 keywords, one for each bullet, plus one detail or example.
  • Speak for the full 2 minutes. Stopping early limits your Fluency score.
  • Tell it like a short story: set the scene, give one specific moment, then explain why it mattered.
  • Answer the last bullet properly. "Explain why…" is where you show range: reasons, feelings and contrasts.

Cue cards with model answers

These are common Part 2 topic types, with original model answers written by PrepEve trainers. Read the card, plan for one minute, speak for two, then compare with the model.

Describe a person who has influenced you

You should say:

  • who this person is
  • how you know them
  • what they did or said
  • and explain why they influenced you
Show a band 8 model answer

I'd like to talk about my school maths teacher, Mrs Kapoor. I first met her in Class 9, when I was honestly scared of numbers. What made her different was that she never just gave us the method; she asked us to explain our thinking out loud, even when it was wrong. One afternoon I got a geometry problem completely wrong in front of the class, and instead of correcting me she asked, "Where exactly did your reasoning change direction?" That one question taught me to look for the step where a mistake begins, not just the wrong answer. I still use that habit today, whether I'm checking a report at work or practising IELTS essays. So she influenced me less through what she taught and more through how she made me think.

Describe a place you would like to visit

You should say:

  • where it is
  • how you know about it
  • what you would do there
  • and explain why you want to go
Show a band 8 model answer

A place I'd really like to visit is the Rocky Mountains in Canada, especially Banff. I first saw photos of it in a friend's travel album, with turquoise lakes surrounded by snow-covered peaks, and I couldn't believe the colour was real. If I went, I'd spend a few days hiking the shorter trails around Lake Louise and maybe take the gondola up for the view. I'd also like to try a canoe on one of the lakes early in the morning, before the crowds arrive. The main reason I want to go is that I've lived in cities all my life, and I'm curious how it feels to be somewhere that quiet and that big. It would also be a good way to see a part of Canada beyond the cities people usually talk about.

Describe a skill you learned that took a long time

You should say:

  • what the skill is
  • when you started learning it
  • how you learned it
  • and explain why it took so long
Show a band 8 model answer

The skill I'd pick is driving. I started learning about four years ago, when I was nineteen. My father taught me at first, early on Sunday mornings when the roads were empty, and later I joined a driving school for proper lessons. It took a long time mainly because of confidence rather than technique. I understood the clutch and gears within a week, but in real traffic I panicked and stalled at signals, so I avoided practising. What finally helped was breaking it into small goals: one week just on the ring road, the next week parking, and so on. It took almost a year before I felt calm behind the wheel. Looking back, it taught me that slow progress is still progress, which is also how I see my English preparation now.

Describe a time you helped someone

You should say:

  • who you helped
  • what the situation was
  • how you helped
  • and explain how you felt about it
Show a band 8 model answer

Last year I helped my neighbour's son, Arjun, prepare for a college interview. He was very nervous because it was his first formal interview, and his English was good but he went silent whenever he was asked about himself. We met three evenings in a row. I played the interviewer, asked him typical questions, and recorded his answers on my phone so he could hear how often he paused or said "actually". By the third evening his answers were shorter, clearer and much more confident. He called me after the interview to say it went well, and a few weeks later he got the place. I felt genuinely proud, probably more than if it had been my own interview, because I could see how much difference a little practice made to him.

Describe a piece of technology you use every day

You should say:

  • what it is
  • how often you use it
  • what you use it for
  • and explain how your life would be different without it
Show a band 8 model answer

The piece of technology I can't do without is my smartphone. I probably check it fifty times a day, from the alarm in the morning to reading before I sleep. I use it for the obvious things like calls and messages, but it's also my bank, my map, my notebook and, at the moment, my IELTS study tool, since I practise listening with podcasts on my way to work. Without it, daily life would be much slower. I'd need cash for small payments, I'd get lost in unfamiliar areas, and I'd lose those small pockets of study time during the commute. On the other hand, I think I'd sleep better and probably read more books, so it wouldn't be entirely negative.

Describe a book you enjoyed reading

You should say:

  • what the book is
  • when you read it
  • what it is about
  • and explain why you enjoyed it
Show a band 8 model answer

A book I really enjoyed is "Atomic Habits" by James Clear, which I read about two years ago during a long train journey. It's a non-fiction book about how very small changes, repeated every day, add up to big results over time. The idea that stayed with me is that instead of setting huge goals, you should design your environment so the good habit is the easy one. For example, if you want to read more, keep the book somewhere you will see it every day. I enjoyed it because it was practical rather than motivational; every chapter ended with something I could try the next day. I actually used one of its ideas, studying for just twenty minutes after dinner, to build my IELTS routine.

Mistakes that cost marks

  • Memorised answers. Examiners notice, and they can lower your score.
  • Long pauses while searching for a "big" word. A simpler word said fluently scores better.
  • Talking in general instead of describing one specific example.

Speaking is easier to improve with feedback. A trainer can hear habits you can't, such as fillers, flat intonation or repeated structures. Read more in how to score 8 in IELTS Speaking and see IELTS speaking coaching.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I speak in IELTS Speaking Part 2?
Up to 2 minutes. The examiner will stop you at 2 minutes. Aim to keep talking until then.
Can I memorise cue card answers?
Do not memorise full answers. Examiners are trained to spot them. Prepare ideas and vocabulary for common topic types instead, and practise speaking freely.
Do I get time to prepare?
Yes, one minute, with paper and pencil to make notes.
Are these the actual exam cue cards?
They are common topic types used for practice. The real test uses many different topics, so practise the skill of answering any card, not specific questions.
