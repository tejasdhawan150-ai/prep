Learning French for Canada PR While Working Full-Time

Yes, you can learn French for Canada PR while working full-time. Plan for about 4–5 hours a week: two or three short live classes, daily 15-minute listening, and weekend revision. In our experience, most working adults starting from zero reach NCLC 7 in 9–14 months at that pace.

What does a realistic weekly French plan look like around office hours?

Short, regular sessions beat one long weekend block. Here is a plan of about 4–5 hours a week that fits a 9-to-6 job.

Sample weekly French study plan for a full-time worker
DayWhat to doTime
MondayLive evening class60–90 min
TuesdayCommute listening + 10 min vocabulary review25 min
WednesdayLive evening class, or the recording if work runs late60–90 min
ThursdaySpeak aloud for 10 min on a daily topic; record yourself15 min
FridayRest, or light listening0–15 min
WeekendWeekend class or revision; one short writing task60–90 min

Two rules make this plan work. First, protect the live classes like work meetings. Second, keep the small daily tasks tiny, so a tiring day never breaks your streak.

How do you use evening and weekend batches and recordings?

Choose one batch slot you can attend every week, and use recordings only as a backup, not a replacement.

  • Pick your slot honestly. PrepEve runs morning, evening and weekend French batches. If your evenings are unpredictable, a morning or weekend batch may be the steadier choice.
  • Use recordings for missed classes. Every class is recorded. If a meeting runs late, watch the recording before the next class so you don't fall behind.
  • Use recordings for revision. Re-watch only the part you found hard, not the whole class.
  • Bring questions. Note down what you didn't understand from a recording and ask in the next live class.

Why should working learners focus on speaking and listening for TEF and TCF?

Because they are the skills busy learners practise least, and each one counts separately for NCLC.

TEF Canada and TCF Canada test listening, reading, writing and speaking. IRCC converts each skill to an NCLC level. To get the French bonus, you need NCLC 7 in all four, so one weak skill holds everything back. Read TEF Canada vs TCF Canada to choose your test, and what NCLC levels mean.

  • Listening: use your commute. Canadian and French radio, podcasts and news clips at natural speed.
  • Speaking: speak in every live class, even with mistakes. Record 2-minute answers at home and compare them with your trainer's model.
  • Reading and writing: easier to fit into short gaps: a short article at lunch, a short message on the weekend.

What are realistic milestones toward NCLC 7?

In our experience, most working adults starting from zero need around 9–14 months at 4–5 hours a week to reach NCLC 7, including test preparation. If you already have some French, it is shorter.

  1. First months — foundations: pronunciation, core grammar, everyday phrases. You can introduce yourself and handle simple conversations.
  2. Middle stage — independence: you talk about work, plans and opinions, and follow slower news and podcasts.
  3. Later stage — B2 level, where NCLC 7 sits: you can argue a point, follow natural-speed speech and write organised texts.
  4. Final stretch — test preparation: TEF or TCF task practice, timed mocks and feedback on your weakest skill.

Your own timeline depends on your start and your hours. The free demo class gives you a personal estimate.

According to IRCC, NCLC 7 or higher in all four French skills gives up to 50 additional CRS points (50 if your English is CLB 5 or higher, 25 if CLB 4 or lower). Learning French does not guarantee an invitation. See French for Canada PR for the full picture.
